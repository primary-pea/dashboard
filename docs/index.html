Policies are written. Anaemia is measured.

Across 49 sub-Saharan African countries the number of nutrition policies on the books grew roughly tenfold between 2000 and 2015. Anaemia in women aged 15–49 fell over those years — and has risen every year since, while the policy count stopped moving. This page lets you check that claim country by country.

Having more policies does not predict doing better

Each dot is a country. Across the bottom: how many anaemia policies it has on the books, averaged over the last years of the panel. Up the side: how far its anaemia sits from what its income, health burden and geography predict. Below the line is better than predicted. The cloud has no tilt — that is the finding.

      Where attention should go

      The team's concern score over four women's nutrition indicators: a country is flagged for sitting in the worst quarter and for failing to improve, and its percentile is added. Because the weighting is a judgement call, the last column shows how often a country still lands in the worst ten when the four weights are drawn at random 500 times. A short bar means the ranking depends on the weights; a full bar means it does not.

      Every way we could have cut it

      336 defensible specifications — different policy measures, lags, income controls, control sets and estimators — each re-fitted and sorted by its estimate. A dot that clears the zero line is a specification whose confidence interval excludes no effect.
